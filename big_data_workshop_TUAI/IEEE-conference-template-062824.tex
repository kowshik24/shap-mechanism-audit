\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models predict equipment failures in industrial systems, but whether their explanations reflect the physical failure mechanisms is rarely tested statistically. We propose an audit that measures, for each known failure mechanism, how much SHAP attribution falls on the mechanism's trigger features, how specific that concentration is to the mechanism, and whether any other feature takes unusual attribution. Explanations are computed out of sample, and each statistic is compared with up to 1,000 models retrained on shuffled labels. On the AI4I 2020 dataset, a tuned XGBoost classifier (test F1 = 0.89) shows significant alignment for three of four physical mechanisms across five seeds, against a null re-tuned on every permutation, with a random forest, and with interventional TreeSHAP; a neural network explained by Integrated Gradients or KernelSHAP shows weaker alignment. The tool-wear verdict depends on the SHAP formulation, which we trace mainly to the reference distribution against which attributions are measured. A planted shortcut sensor shows that models can keep high accuracy while relying on a misleading feature, that weak shortcuts escape detection, and that at 300,000 observations only the shortcut statistic still detects a strong one. Predictive accuracy alone therefore cannot establish the physical relevance of explanations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
% Prakrit
%Context and Importance
Predictive maintenance uses machine-condition data to anticipate faults and schedule maintenance, reducing unplanned downtime \cite{cummins2024survey}. Models range from decision trees to gradient-boosted ensembles and neural networks \cite{bukhsh2019predictive,jalali2020interpretability}, and the more complex ones are difficult to interpret \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Knowing which sensor readings drive a prediction helps maintenance teams assess an alarm \cite{steurtewagen2021adding}, and explanation methods such as LIME \cite{ribeiro2016lime,bukhsh2019predictive} and SHAP \cite{steurtewagen2021adding,gawde2024explainable} are now common in the field, including studies of how preprocessing and model complexity affect explanation quality \cite{ndao2025improving}.

%Specific Problem and its relevance
High predictive performance can coexist with reliance on shortcuts or correlations unrelated to the intended task \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}. In predictive maintenance, this raises the question of whether explanations emphasize the expected features for each failure type. SHAP assigns feature contributions to individual predictions \cite{lundberg2017shap}, but these contributions do not by themselves establish the physical relevance of those inputs \cite{janzing2020feature}. Evaluating feature attributions is difficult when a suitable ground-truth reference is unavailable \cite{zhou2022attribute}. Assessing mechanism alignment therefore requires information about which failure mechanism occurred and which features its rule depends on. Label-permutation methods provide a statistical reference for evaluating feature importance when the association between inputs and outcomes is disrupted \cite{altmann2010permutation}. The question is therefore not only which features receive attribution, but whether their attribution is consistent with the recorded mechanism and exceeds that observed under this reference.

% Evaluation setting, prior work, and research gap
In this work, we use the AI4I 2020 predictive maintenance dataset, a synthetic dataset containing machine operating measurements, an overall failure label, and separate failure-type labels \cite{uci2020ai4i}. Its recorded failure types and documented generation rules provide an external reference for evaluating mechanism alignment. Previous work on this dataset has assessed explanation quality using selected examples \cite{matzka2020explainable}, while subsequent work has used SHAP to interpret feature importance \cite{milling2025shap}. More recently, Obeten and Jimoh have quantitatively compared SHAP and LIME feature rankings on AI4I 2020 \cite{obeten2026agreement}. Agreement between explanation methods, however, does not by itself establish alignment with a failure mechanism. We build on these studies by testing mechanism-level attribution on held-out observations against a reference obtained from models retrained on shuffled labels. We also examine whether the audit detects reliance on a deliberately fabricated feature.


% Overview of the proposed approach
Our audit trains a classifier on the overall failure label only and uses the recorded failure types to group its SHAP explanations by mechanism (\cref{fig:pipeline}). Three statistics measure the attribution share on a mechanism's expected trigger features, how much larger that share is than on other failures, and the largest excess share taken by any other feature. Explanations come from five-fold cross-fitting, so no observation is explained by a model trained on it, and each statistic is compared with pipelines retrained on shuffled labels, with Holm correction across mechanisms.

% Contributions build on the approach, progressing from method to evaluation and findings.
Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A mechanism-level SHAP audit with held-out explanations and a retrained shuffled-label null; we show that a random-subset baseline can approve a model trained on meaningless labels.
    \item A robustness study across seeds, features, a re-tuned null, two tree ensembles, three explainer families, and the explainer's background, locating the tool-wear disagreement in the reference distribution.
    \item A planted-shortcut study showing that high accuracy can conceal shortcut reliance and that weak shortcuts escape detection.
    \item A scaling study up to 300,000 observations (runtime up to one million), showing that only the shortcut statistic catches a strong shortcut at the largest size.
    \item A benchmark of seven classifiers with confidence intervals, and released code, models, and reproduction scripts.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work and Background}
% Prakrit
\label{sec:related}

Predictive maintenance studies use decision trees, random forests, and gradient-boosted trees \cite{bukhsh2019predictive}, as well as support vector methods and neural networks \cite{jalali2020interpretability}. Complex models require post-hoc explanation, and surveys review approaches ranging from inherently interpretable models to surrogate models, LIME, and SHAP \cite{cummins2024survey,vollert2021interpretable}. Local explanations examine individual predictions, whereas global summaries describe recurring patterns; in maintenance, explaining a single alarm and understanding a model's general reliance on sensor measurements are related but different tasks. Our audit sits between the two: it aggregates local attributions within each recorded failure mechanism.

Steurtewagen and Van den Poel~\cite{steurtewagen2021adding} combine sensor and report data with an XGBoost model and use Shapley values to support the diagnosis of machinery breakdowns in the petrochemical industry, and Jalali et al.~\cite{jalali2020interpretability} compare LIME, SHAP, and attribute-wise explanations on the PHM08-CMAPSS data. Closest to our setting, Matzka~\cite{matzka2020explainable} assessed explanation quality on AI4I~2020 against its known failure mechanisms using five examples from each of four physical failure types, a direct precedent for comparing explanations with the features expected to matter. Later work interpreted SHAP rankings on the same data \cite{milling2025shap}, and Obeten and Jimoh measured SHAP--LIME agreement \cite{obeten2026agreement}. We extend this direction from selected examples and method agreement to a statistical test over all held-out observations.

SHAP assigns each feature an additive contribution that, together with a baseline value, reconstructs the explained output \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Covert et al.~\cite{covert2021explaining} organize removal-based explanations by how they handle unavailable features, which model behavior they explain, and how they summarize each feature's influence, showing that the assumption used to represent unavailable features is part of the explanation's definition. Shapley variants accordingly differ, conditioning on the remaining features, marginalizing over a background distribution, or substituting a single baseline \cite{sundararajan2020many}, and recent surveys show that these choices answer different questions about the model and the data \cite{chen2023algorithms,chen2020true}, particularly when inputs are correlated \cite{aas2021explaining,kumar2020problems}. For tree ensembles, path-dependent TreeSHAP weights each path by its training cover, whereas interventional TreeSHAP averages over an explicit background sample \cite{lundberg2020treeshap}. Faster exact algorithms, such as Linear TreeSHAP \cite{yu2022linear} and the quadrature-based Quadrature-TreeSHAP \cite{wettenstein2026quadrature}, accelerate the path-dependent computation while retaining its formulation. The choice matters here because engineered features share information with the measurements they are built from, and \cref{sec:variants} shows that it can decide a mechanism's verdict; agreement with trigger features must therefore be read in light of the explanation formulation, not as proof of physical causation.

Explanation-evaluation research offers complementary checks. Benchmarks with known ground truth, such as XAI-Bench \cite{liu2021synthetic}, BAM \cite{yang2019bam}, and CLEVR-XAI \cite{arras2022clevr}, test whether attributions recover planted relevance; OpenXAI evaluates faithfulness, stability, and fairness \cite{agarwal2022openxai}; and ROAR \cite{hooker2019roar} and its retraining-free successor ROAD \cite{rong2022road} remove highly attributed inputs and measure the change in performance. Global sensitivity analysis based on the Hilbert--Schmidt independence criterion (HSIC) measures statistical dependence between inputs and outputs without a removal step \cite{daveiga2015global}. These approaches characterize the model or its explainer; assessing agreement with a failure mechanism additionally requires an external reference, such as the documented failure rules that AI4I~2020 provides.

Randomization provides the reference we use. Label-permutation tests assess classifier performance \cite{ojala2010permutation}, and outcome permutations calibrate feature-importance measures \cite{altmann2010permutation}. Some visually convincing saliency methods are insensitive to randomized parameters or labels \cite{adebayo2018sanity} or unreliable under simple input transformations \cite{kindermans2019unreliability}, and post-hoc explanations such as LIME and SHAP can be deliberately manipulated \cite{slack2020fooling}, so explanations need explicit controls. Research on shortcut learning gives a further reason: models can exploit predictive associations that do not represent the intended mechanism \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}.

Together, these studies motivate testing both whether explanations agree with known failure mechanisms and whether they reveal unexpected feature reliance. We address both with a mechanism-level audit of held-out SHAP attributions against documented trigger features, tested against models retrained on shuffled labels with correction for multiple comparisons, and with a planted-shortcut experiment that measures what the audit detects and what it misses.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:background}

A model may predict a failure correctly without relying on the conditions that caused it. We therefore ask: \emph{when a particular failure occurs, does the model attribute more importance to the features in that failure's physical rule?}

Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i,z_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote a dataset where
$x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ contains the input features, $y_i\in\{0,1\}$
indicates overall equipment failure, and $z_i\in\{0,1\}^{M}$ records
$M$ individual failure mechanisms. A classifier $f$ is trained using
only $(x_i,y_i)$; the mechanism indicators are reserved for evaluating
its explanations.

For each mechanism $m$, domain knowledge identifies a set $E_m$ of
\emph{trigger features} involved in its physical rule. We define
$S_m=\{i:z_{i,m}=1\}$ as the observations where mechanism $m$ occurs
and $F=\{i:y_i=1\}$ as all observations labeled as failures. Let
$\phi_j(x_i)$ denote the SHAP attribution of feature $j$ for observation
$i$.

The audit tests whether attribution concentrates on the expected trigger features, whether that concentration is specific to the mechanism, and whether unrelated features receive unusual attribution (\cref{fig:pipeline}). It measures agreement with known failure rules, not physical causation.

\subsection{Dataset and Failure Mechanisms}
\label{sec:data}

AI4I~2020~\cite{matzka2020explainable} is a synthetic industrial
predictive maintenance dataset representing a milling process. It
contains 10{,}000 observations, each recording product quality
(\texttt{L}, \texttt{M}, or \texttt{H}), air and process temperatures,
rotational speed, torque, tool wear, an overall failure label, and
five failure-mechanism indicators. Only 339 observations (3.4\%)
are labeled as failures, making the prediction task highly imbalanced.

The dataset describes five mechanisms: tool wear failure (TWF),
heat dissipation failure (HDF), power failure (PWF), overstrain
failure (OSF), and random failure (RNF). The overall failure label
is intended to follow the logical OR of these mechanisms:

\begin{equation}
y_i^{\mathrm{OR}}
=
\mathbb{I}
\left(
\sum_{m=1}^{M}z_{i,m}\geq 1
\right),
\label{eq:failure_or}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. \Cref{tab:mechanisms}
lists the documented rules and the trigger features used in the audit.

\paragraph{Overlapping mechanisms and label discrepancies}
The released annotations do not always follow the intended OR
relationship. Twenty-four observations have multiple active mechanism
flags; these are valid overlapping conditions rather than labeling
errors. However, nine observations are labeled as failures without
any active flag, while 18 of the 19 RNF-flagged observations have
an overall failure label of zero.

We train on the released overall labels. The primary audit includes every observation where the evaluated mechanism is active, and we repeat it on observations with exactly one active flag to remove ambiguity from overlaps. RNF has no trigger features and is examined only descriptively.

\subsection{Feature Construction}
\label{sec:features}

Several mechanisms depend on combinations of sensor readings rather
than individual measurements. We therefore construct four features
from the original inputs: mechanical power
$P=\tau(2\pi n/60)$, temperature difference
$\Delta T=T_{\mathrm{process}}-T_{\mathrm{air}}$,
overstrain $O=w\tau$, and overstrain ratio $Q=O/L(t)$,
where $\tau$, $n$, and $w$ denote torque, rotational speed,
and tool wear, respectively. The type-specific limit $L(t)$
is 11{,}000, 12{,}000, or 13{,}000 for product types
\texttt{L}, \texttt{M}, and \texttt{H}.

They encode the interactions in the HDF, PWF, and OSF rules without
using any labels, but they are correlated with the raw measurements,
so we repeat the audit on the original features only (\cref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Explanation Audit Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

We evaluate attribution using three complementary statistics.
For feature $j$ and observation set $A$, let

\begin{equation}
I_{j}(A)
=
\frac{1}{|A|}
\sum_{i\in A}|\phi_j(x_i)|,
\qquad
s_j(A)
=
\frac{I_{j}(A)}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}I_{k}(A)}
\label{eq:mean_shap}
\end{equation}

denote the average absolute SHAP contribution and the attribution
share of feature $j$, where $\phi_j(x_i)$ is computed by the
cross-fitted model that did not train on observation $i$
(\cref{sec:oos}). We use absolute values because positive and
negative contributions could otherwise cancel. The fraction of
attribution assigned to the expected trigger features of
mechanism $m$ is

\begin{equation}
r_m(A)
=
\sum_{j\in E_m}s_j(A).
\label{eq:relevant_fraction}
\end{equation}

From these quantities, we define three statistics:

\begin{align}
R_m &= r_m(S_m),
\label{eq:share}\\
D_m &= r_m(S_m)-r_m(F\setminus S_m),
\label{eq:spec}\\
C_m &= \max_{j\notin E_m}
\left[
s_j(S_m)-s_j(F\setminus S_m)
\right],
\label{eq:shortcut}
\end{align}

$R_m$ measures how much attribution goes to the trigger features on mechanism $m$'s observations, $D_m$ whether that share is larger than on other failures, and $C_m$ the largest mechanism-specific increase among features outside $E_m$. A large $C_m$ raises concern about reliance on another feature but does not prove the feature causally irrelevant.

For example, on the 95 PWF observations, power, torque, and speed
have mean $|\phi|$ of 4.96, 1.93, and 1.33 (\cref{fig:shap}a), out of
11.82 over all twelve inputs, so $R_{\mathrm{PWF}}\approx0.70$.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Explanations}
\label{sec:oos}

Explanations of training observations may reflect overfitting, so we use five-fold cross-fitting~\cite{chernozhukov2018double}: the full pipeline is fitted on four stratified folds and explains the fifth, giving every observation an explanation from a model that did not train on it. \Cref{sec:robust} contrasts this with in-sample SHAP.

\subsection{Statistical Significance}
\label{sec:nulls}

A high score alone is not evidence of alignment: mechanism observations often have extreme sensor values that receive large SHAP values even when the model has learned nothing, which makes a comparison against random failure observations unreliable (\cref{sec:naive}).

We instead build a \emph{shuffled-label null}: we permute the
overall failure labels, keeping sensors and mechanism flags, and
repeat the full preprocessing, oversampling, training,
cross-fitting, and explanation procedure with the hyperparameters
selected on the original data. Because carrying these hyperparameters
over could itself shape the null, we also rebuild it with the full
hyperparameter search repeated on every permutation (\cref{sec:robust}).

For an observed value $T$ and null values $T^{(1)},\ldots,T^{(K)}$,
the upper-tail Monte Carlo $p$-value is

\begin{equation}
p=
\frac{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\mathbb{I}(T^{(k)}\geq T)}
{K+1}.
\label{eq:pvalue}
\end{equation}

The primary audit uses $K=1{,}000$, giving a minimum possible
$p$-value of $1/1001\approx0.001$. We apply Holm's
correction~\cite{holm1979simple} across the four physical
mechanisms for each statistic and use $\alpha=0.05$.
To complement the $p$-values, we report a null-standardized effect
size $z=(T-\bar{T}_{0})/s_{0}$, where $\bar{T}_{0}$ and $s_{0}$ are
the mean and standard deviation of the null values, and 95\%
percentile bootstrap intervals for $R_m$ and $D_m$ obtained by
resampling observations within $S_m$ and $F\setminus S_m$
(5{,}000 resamples). The bootstrap holds the cross-fitted models
fixed, so it reflects observation sampling only; variability from
refitting is captured by the null.

We combine the three tests into one decision rule. A mechanism
is \emph{aligned} when both $R_m$ and $D_m$ are significantly
larger than their null distributions and $C_m$ is not; it is
\emph{flagged} when $C_m$ is significant, whatever $R_m$ and
$D_m$ show, and \emph{not aligned} otherwise. The shortcut test
can therefore overturn a positive verdict, which matters at
large $n$, where a narrow null lets $R_m$ pass even when a
non-trigger feature takes much of the attribution
(\cref{sec:scale}). A flag names the responsible feature and its
excess share, which should be inspected before acting on it.
A non-significant shortcut result means that the audit did not
detect such an increase; it does not establish that shortcuts
are absent.
RNF is excluded from the physical-alignment tests because
it has no expected trigger features.

\subsection{Model Training and Evaluation}
\label{sec:training}

We split the data into stratified training, validation, and test sets (70/20/10), fitting scaling and one-hot encoding on the training data. To handle the 3.4\% failure rate, we compare SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote}, Borderline-SMOTE~\cite{han2005borderline}, and ADASYN~\cite{he2008adasyn} by the validation F1 of a default XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}. We compare seven classifiers: logistic regression, RBF-kernel SVM, $k$-NN ($k=7$), a decision tree (depth 10), a random forest (300 trees, depth 12), XGBoost (300 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.1), and an MLP (64 and 32 hidden units), with library defaults otherwise (scikit-learn 1.9.1, XGBoost 3.4.1, PyTorch 2.14.0; seed 42). The best model, XGBoost, is tuned by a 40-iteration randomized search with five-fold cross-validation on F1 (depth 3--8, 200--600 trees, learning rate 0.01--0.2, subsampling 0.6--1.0, minimum child weight 1--5, $\gamma$ 0--0.3), with oversampling inside each fold, and its threshold maximizes validation F1. We report failure-class precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, Brier score, and expected calibration error, with Clopper--Pearson intervals for precision and recall, 5{,}000 stratified bootstrap resamples otherwise, and five-fold cross-validation at threshold 0.5.

\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:setup}

The primary audit uses $K=1{,}000$ and takes about 12 minutes on eight CPU cores; variants use $K=200$ unless stated, the shortcut sweep $K=500$.

\paragraph{Robustness checks}
We repeat the audit with a null re-tuned on every permutation, across four additional random seeds, on
single-mechanism observations, without engineered features,
and using in-sample rather than held-out SHAP values. We also
compare path-dependent TreeSHAP with interventional TreeSHAP,
using background samples of 100, 500, and 1{,}000 training
observations per fold, drawn uniformly at random from that
fold's original (not oversampled) training observations with
up to three background seeds, and repeat the audit with a random
forest. To examine the TWF result, we further draw the interventional
background only from non-failures, only from failures, or from the
oversampled training data, and run the interventional audit on the
original features alone. Finally, to test whether the audit depends
on tree ensembles or TreeSHAP, we audit the MLP of \cref{sec:training}
with two non-tree explainers: KernelSHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, a
model-agnostic Shapley estimator (25 training observations as
background, 1{,}000 coalitions per observation), and Integrated
Gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}, a gradient-based method
(baseline equal to the mean of 100 training observations, 1{,}024
steps, completeness checked to within 5\%). Both explain the MLP's
logit, and every null model retrains the MLP on permuted labels and
is explained in the same way. Every variant uses a matching null.

\paragraph{Controlled shortcut experiment}
To test whether the audit detects reliance on an unrelated
but predictive feature, we introduce an artificial sensor

\begin{equation}
s_i=z_{i,\mathrm{HDF}}+\epsilon_i,
\qquad
\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).
\label{eq:shortcut_sensor}
\end{equation}

This noisy copy of the HDF flag has no physical role and is
excluded from $E_{\mathrm{HDF}}$; we decrease $\sigma$ from 2 to 0.1
to make it increasingly predictive.

\paragraph{Scaling experiment}
We generate new observations using the documented AI4I~2020
process~\cite{matzka2020explainable}, without copying released
rows. The sensor distributions follow the documented process,
and HDF, PWF, and OSF use their published rules. Tool wear
increases until a lifetime drawn from $U(200,240)$\,min,
where TWF occurs with probability 0.4. The generated failure
rate is 3.5--4.0\%, compared with 3.4\% in the release.

We evaluate $n\in\{10^4,3\cdot10^4,10^5,3\cdot10^5\}$
with three generation seeds for the first three sizes and
one for the largest. Each setting is tested without a
shortcut and with shortcut noise
$\sigma\in\{0.35,0.25\}$. The scaling experiments use
$K=200$ null models, and the generator is included in
the released code.

\paragraph{Why simulated data}
AI4I~2020 documents its failure mechanisms, a reference rarely available in real data. The classifier sees only the overall label and may learn other relationships, so agreement with the rules is not automatic; the shuffled-label, shortcut, and raw-feature experiments test whether the audit distinguishes genuine from chance or shortcut agreement.

\paragraph{HDF, PWF, and OSF}
All three mechanisms pass the relevant and specificity tests,
with Holm-adjusted $p=0.004$ for each. Their trigger features
receive substantially more attribution on the corresponding
mechanism observations than on other failures, with specificity
scores between 0.33 and 0.43 and effect sizes of $z=5.7$--$8.4$.
The relevant-share effect is smallest for HDF ($z=3.4$): its
four trigger features include both temperatures and rotational
speed, on which even shuffled-label models place much
attribution (null mean 0.56).
The bootstrap intervals are narrow (half-widths of 0.02--0.03)
compared with the null standard deviations (0.04--0.06), so
uncertainty in these verdicts comes mainly from model
refitting rather than from the finite number of mechanism
observations. No statistically significant
shortcut is detected in the primary audit.

\paragraph{TWF}
Tool wear failure does not pass either alignment test
($p_R=0.201$, $p_D=0.077$). Although tool wear is its most
important feature, it receives only 19\% of the attribution
on TWF observations, compared with 15\% under the null.
The documented TWF mechanism includes a random failure point
within a tool-wear interval, and only 46 observations carry
this flag. Both factors may make its attribution harder
to distinguish from the null.

\subsection{Robustness of Mechanism Alignment}
\label{sec:robust}

\paragraph{Random seeds and overlapping mechanisms}
HDF, PWF, and OSF pass both alignment tests across all
five random seeds and when the analysis is restricted
to observations with a single active mechanism.
TWF fails both tests in every seed.

\paragraph{Re-tuned null}
Repeating the 40-iteration hyperparameter search on each of
200 permutations gives a wider null than carrying over the
hyperparameters selected on the real labels: the search on
shuffled labels mostly selects shallow trees (depth at most 4
in 115 of 200 permutations), and the null 95th percentile of
$R_{\mathrm{OSF}}$ rises from 0.46 to 0.62. The verdicts are
unchanged. HDF, PWF, and OSF pass both tests and TWF fails
both ($p_R=0.453$, $p_D=0.368$), but the OSF relevant-share
margin narrows: 4 of 200 re-tuned null models reach the
observed 0.672, giving an adjusted $p=0.0498$. The carried-over
null is therefore the less conservative reference, and the
OSF relevant-share result should be read as borderline under
the stricter one.

\paragraph{In-sample versus out-of-sample explanations}
When explanations are calculated on observations used
for training, all four mechanisms pass, including TWF
($p=0.004$). Its relevant score increases from 0.19
with out-of-sample SHAP to 0.35 with in-sample SHAP.
Explaining training observations thus gives an optimistic verdict.

\paragraph{Original features only}
Removing the engineered features changes the verdict for HDF, which retains significant specificity but no longer passes the relevant-share test. PWF and OSF continue to pass both tests, although OSF specificity is close to the threshold ($p=0.048$). TWF still fails. PWF and OSF alignment therefore does not depend on the derived features, whereas HDF's does, consistent with the difficulty of representing a temperature-difference rule with separate temperatures in a tree model.

\subsection{Why the Choice of Null Matters}
\label{sec:naive}

An audit should reject a model that learned nothing. A classifier trained on shuffled labels fails our alignment tests, but compared with random failure observations under the same model, all four mechanisms appear significant (adjusted $p=0.002$): the naive comparison approves a model trained on meaningless labels, because mechanism observations have unusual sensor values that receive large attributions regardless. Retraining under shuffled labels is therefore necessary.

\subsection{Can the Audit Detect a Shortcut?}
\label{sec:sweep}

\Cref{fig:sweep} shows attribution shifting from the HDF trigger features to the planted sensor as its noise decreases.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/shortcut_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Effect of an injected shortcut on HDF attribution. As noise
decreases, the artificial sensor takes a larger share and the trigger
features less. Markers indicate a failed relevant-share test.}
\label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

For weak shortcuts ($\sigma\geq0.4$, at most 8\% of the attribution), HDF passes. At 13\% ($\sigma=0.35$) the relevant-share test still passes ($p=0.044$), and it fails from 21\% ($\sigma=0.30$). The shortcut statistic becomes significant at about 35\% ($\sigma\leq0.25$), and the specificity test fails at about 42\%. Test F1 stays between 0.84 and 0.92 throughout, so the model keeps good accuracy while increasingly relying on an unrelated feature. PWF and OSF pass in every setting. Weak shortcuts thus go undetected, and the shortcut statistic is less sensitive than the relevant-share test at moderate strengths.

\subsection{Effect of the Model and SHAP Method}
\label{sec:variants}

HDF, PWF, and OSF pass the alignment tests using both path-dependent and interventional TreeSHAP, and their verdicts remain unchanged with a random forest.

\paragraph{Beyond trees}
The audit itself needs only an attribution matrix, so it runs unchanged on the MLP (test F1 0.76) with KernelSHAP and Integrated Gradients, each against 200 retrained MLP nulls (\cref{tab:robust}). With Integrated Gradients, HDF and OSF are aligned (adjusted $p\leq0.030$ for both tests), PWF passes the relevant-share test narrowly ($p=0.0498$) but not specificity ($p=0.10$), and TWF fails. With KernelSHAP, no mechanism is aligned; only PWF specificity is significant. Two factors make these verdicts weaker than for XGBoost. First, shuffled-label MLPs already place much attribution on the trigger features, so the null means are high (e.g., $R_{\mathrm{PWF}}$ null mean 0.60--0.63 versus 0.35 for XGBoost), likely because a smooth network trained on noise still spreads attribution over the inputs that the PWF and OSF rules use. Second, the MLP is a weaker classifier, and KernelSHAP adds sampling error from its 25-row background and 1{,}000 of the 4{,}094 coalitions. We cannot separate these factors here, but the result shows why the shuffled-label null is needed: under KernelSHAP, the raw relevant shares of HDF, PWF, and OSF (0.47--0.67) look like alignment, yet none exceeds what an MLP without failure knowledge produces.

TWF behaves differently. It passes both tests with interventional TreeSHAP across the tested background sizes and seeds, with relevant scores between 0.45 and 0.47. However, it fails with path-dependent TreeSHAP and the random forest. Tool wear is strongly correlated with the engineered overstrain features overall (Spearman 0.92), and different SHAP formulations distribute attribution among dependent features differently~\cite{aas2021explaining}. Correlation alone does not explain the flip, however: on TWF observations the correlation is only 0.13, and path-dependent TWF alignment also fails without the engineered features (\cref{tab:robust}). Interventional TreeSHAP also passes TWF on the raw features alone ($R=0.58$, $p=0.020$).

The flip is better explained by the reference distribution against which each formulation measures a feature's contribution. Path-dependent TreeSHAP weights tree paths by the training cover, which reflects the oversampled data the model was fitted on, whereas our interventional background is drawn from the original training observations, about 97\% of which are non-failures. We therefore repeat the interventional audit with three backgrounds (\cref{tab:robust}), each with a matching null. A non-failure background reproduces the default result ($R_{\mathrm{TWF}}=0.46$). A background drawn from the oversampled training data lowers $R_{\mathrm{TWF}}$ to 0.23, close to the path-dependent 0.19, although TWF still passes narrowly ($p_R=0.045$). A failure-only background lowers it to 0.08 and also breaks HDF alignment. The attribution therefore answers different questions under different references: against typical operation, tool wear is what distinguishes TWF observations, but against other failures it is not. Thus, the TWF verdict depends mainly on the choice of reference distribution rather than on feature correlation, and remains unresolved in the sense that no single choice is correct for all purposes. For maintenance audits that ask why an observation is abnormal, we recommend a background drawn from normal operation and reporting it alongside the result.

\subsection{Does the Audit Work with More Data?}
\label{sec:scale}

We repeat the audit on generated datasets containing $10^4$ to $3\cdot10^5$ observations. Without an injected shortcut, HDF, PWF, and OSF pass the alignment tests at every evaluated size and seed. TWF continues to fail the relevant-share test, showing that additional observations alone do not resolve its alignment.

The shortcut experiments reveal a different pattern. At $\sigma=0.25$, the artificial sensor receives 27--45\% of HDF attribution. The relevant-share test detects the resulting loss of alignment in all nine runs up to 100{,}000 observations, but not at 300{,}000. At the largest size, the observed relevant score is 0.472, exceeding the null's 95th percentile of 0.417. As the null distribution becomes narrower, a model can remain significantly better than shuffled-label models while relying heavily on a misleading feature.

The shortcut score detects the stronger shortcut in all ten evaluated runs, including at 300{,}000 observations. At that size, its observed value is 0.249, compared with the null's 95th percentile of 0.211. Under the combined decision rule, HDF is therefore flagged rather than aligned in that run; across all experiments, this is the only case in which the shortcut test changes a verdict that $R_m$ and $D_m$ alone would have given. The weaker shortcut at $\sigma=0.35$, which receives 6--20\% of attribution, is not detected reliably.

The shortcut score also flags TWF in 7 of 30 runs from $3\cdot10^4$ observations onward, three of them without an injected sensor, always pointing to air or process temperature with small excess shares (0.03--0.05). The generator draws temperatures independently of tool life, so these flags cannot reflect a genuine shortcut. They likely arise because attribution on TWF observations, which the model captures poorly, is spread more evenly across features than on other failures, and the narrowing null at large $n$ makes this small excess significant. A significant $C_m$ should therefore be read together with the size of the excess share and the identity of the flagged feature.

\paragraph{Computational cost}
The audit requires $5(K+1)$ model fits because each real and randomized model is evaluated through five-fold cross-fitting. A single cross-fitted run takes approximately 1.3 seconds for $10^4$ observations, 31 seconds for $3\cdot10^5$, and 115 seconds for $10^6$ observations on one core, growing roughly linearly with $n$. With eight cores, the complete $K=200$ audit takes about three minutes at $10^4$ observations and approximately one hour at $3\cdot10^5$. The independent randomized runs can be distributed across multiple processors or machines.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study evaluated whether SHAP explanations of predictive
maintenance models align with known physical failure mechanisms.
Using AI4I~2020, we proposed a statistical audit that compares
feature attributions with expected failure conditions and
shuffled-label models. Despite achieving a test F1 of 0.89,
the XGBoost model showed consistent alignment for three failure
mechanisms, while tool wear results depended on the reference
distribution used by the explanation method, and a neural network
showed weaker, explainer-dependent alignment. The audit also revealed that accurate predictions can
rely on misleading features and that simpler evaluation methods
may fail to detect them. These findings highlight the need to
evaluate explanations beyond predictive accuracy to better
assess their agreement with known failure mechanisms.

For practitioners, our results suggest five rules. Explain only
observations the model did not train on. Compare statistics with
models retrained on shuffled labels, not with random subsets of
failures. State the explainer's reference distribution, and for
alarms ask why an observation is abnormal by drawing the background
from normal operation. Report effect sizes and the flagged feature
alongside $p$-values, and treat a mechanism as aligned only when
$R_m$ and $D_m$ pass and $C_m$ does not, since at large $n$ a narrow
null lets $R_m$ pass despite a strong shortcut. Finally, re-tune the
null models when the budget allows, as carried-over hyperparameters
give a less conservative reference.

%======================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits_future}

The audit is evaluated only on simulated AI4I~2020 data, so it
establishes agreement with known simulator rules rather than verified
physical causes, and it requires expert-defined trigger features. The
TWF verdict depends on the explainer's reference distribution, and the
MLP results depend on the explainer; LIME was not evaluated. The
shortcut statistic misses shortcuts below about a third of the
attribution, a studentized variant was less sensitive, and at large
$n$ small excesses can be flagged on poorly learned mechanisms. Under a
null re-tuned on every permutation, OSF's relevant share is borderline
($p=0.0498$), and the bootstrap intervals hold the models fixed. Future
work will apply the audit to real data with recorded failure causes,
such as the Tennessee Eastman Process~\cite{rieth2017tepdata,rieth2018issues},
develop more sensitive shortcut statistics, and add rank-based or
distance-based statistics and dependence-based sensitivity
indices~\cite{daveiga2015global} as complementary checks.
