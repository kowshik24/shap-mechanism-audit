\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models are increasingly used to predict equipment failures in industrial systems, helping reduce downtime and maintenance costs. However, many high-performing models operate as black boxes, making it difficult to understand their predictions. Explainable AI (XAI) methods identify influential features, but whether these explanations align with the underlying physical failure mechanisms remains unclear. To investigate this gap, we propose a statistical framework that evaluates the consistency between feature attributions and known failure mechanisms using three complementary measures. Using the AI4I 2020 dataset, which provides both failure outcomes and mechanism-specific labels, we assess whether observed alignment exceeds chance-level expectations through comparisons with 1,000 models trained on randomly shuffled labels. Experiments show that an XGBoost classifier (test F1 = 0.89) achieves statistically significant alignment for three failure mechanisms across five random seeds and against a null re-tuned on every permutation, with supporting results from a random forest and interventional TreeSHAP; a neural network explained by Integrated Gradients or KernelSHAP shows weaker alignment. However, explanations for tool wear are sensitive to the choice of SHAP formulation, which we trace mainly to the reference distribution against which attributions are measured. Controlled experiments further show that models can achieve strong predictive performance while assigning substantial importance to misleading features, highlighting a potential disconnect between predictive accuracy and explanation reliability. Additionally, some evaluation measures become less sensitive to attribution differences as dataset size increases. These findings demonstrate that predictive accuracy alone cannot establish the physical relevance of model explanations and underscore the importance of statistically evaluating their alignment with known failure mechanisms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} 
% Prakrit
%Context and Importance
Predictive maintenance is a maintenance strategy that uses machine-condition data to help anticipate faults and determine when maintenance should be performed. By identifying developing problems, it can help reduce unplanned downtime \cite{cummins2024survey}. Machine-learning models support this process by analyzing equipment data to predict failures and maintenance needs. These models include decision trees, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, support vector methods, and neural networks \cite{bukhsh2019predictive,jalali2020interpretability}. Complex ensembles and neural networks can be difficult to interpret, making prediction performance alone insufficient for understanding their behavior \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Understanding which sensor readings influence their predictions can help maintenance teams assess an alarm and decide what to investigate \cite{steurtewagen2021adding}. Interpretability approaches used in predictive maintenance include decision trees and local surrogate explanations such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) \cite{ribeiro2016lime,bukhsh2019predictive}, as well as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for interpreting sensor contributions to model predictions \cite{steurtewagen2021adding,gawde2024explainable}. Recent work has also examined how preprocessing and model complexity affect explanation quality in remaining-useful-life prediction \cite{ndao2025improving}.

%Specific Problem and its relevance
High predictive performance can coexist with reliance on shortcuts or correlations unrelated to the intended task \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}. In predictive maintenance, this raises the question of whether explanations emphasize the expected features for each failure type. SHAP assigns feature contributions to individual predictions \cite{lundberg2017shap}, but these contributions do not by themselves establish the physical relevance of those inputs \cite{janzing2020feature}. Evaluating feature attributions is difficult when a suitable ground-truth reference is unavailable \cite{zhou2022attribute}. Assessing mechanism alignment therefore requires information about which failure mechanism occurred and which features its rule depends on. Label-permutation methods provide a statistical reference for evaluating feature importance when the association between inputs and outcomes is disrupted \cite{altmann2010permutation}. The question is therefore not only which features receive attribution, but whether their attribution is consistent with the recorded mechanism and exceeds that observed under this reference.

% Evaluation setting, prior work, and research gap
In this work, we use the AI4I 2020 predictive maintenance dataset, a synthetic dataset containing machine operating measurements, an overall failure label, and separate failure-type labels \cite{uci2020ai4i}. Its recorded failure types and documented generation rules provide an external reference for evaluating mechanism alignment. Previous work on this dataset has assessed explanation quality using selected examples \cite{matzka2020explainable}, while subsequent work has used SHAP to interpret feature importance \cite{milling2025shap}. More recently, Obeten and Jimoh have quantitatively compared SHAP and LIME feature rankings on AI4I 2020 \cite{obeten2026agreement}. Agreement between explanation methods, however, does not by itself establish alignment with a failure mechanism. We build on these studies by testing mechanism-level attribution on held-out observations against a reference obtained from models retrained on shuffled labels. We also examine whether the audit detects reliance on a deliberately fabricated feature.


% Overview of the proposed approach: SHAP audit, statistical testing, and predictive benchmark.
Our approach trains a classifier to predict overall machine failure while withholding the individual failure-type labels from its inputs. We use SHAP because it provides individual feature contributions that can be aggregated by failure type and compared with expected trigger features. TreeSHAP supports efficient attribution computation for the XGBoost and Random Forest models audited in this work \cite{lundberg2020treeshap}. We use the recorded failure-type labels to group these explanations by mechanism. From absolute SHAP values, the audit measures three properties: the attribution share assigned to expected trigger features, how much larger that share is for the relevant mechanism than for other failures, and the largest excess attribution share assigned to a feature outside the expected set. Explanations are computed through five-fold cross-fitting, so each audited observation is explained by a model that was not trained on it. For the primary audit, we construct a reference using 1,000 permutations of the overall failure labels, retraining models and recomputing explanations and statistics for each permutation while retaining the recorded mechanism groups. We apply Holm correction across the four physical mechanisms, separately for each statistic.

% Contributions build on the approach, progressing from method to evaluation and findings.
To extend example-based explanation assessments with a
statistical evaluation of mechanism alignment, we make
the following contributions:
\begin{itemize} 
    \item We propose a mechanism-level SHAP audit with held-out explanations and statistical testing, showing that a random-subset baseline can misleadingly suggest alignment.
    \item We assess robustness across data, model, and explainer settings, including a null re-tuned on every permutation and a neural network explained by KernelSHAP and Integrated Gradients. Heat dissipation, power, and overstrain show alignment for tree ensembles, while tool-wear conclusions depend on the reference distribution of the explainer.
    \item We test fabricated sensors of varying strength, showing that strong predictive performance can conceal shortcut reliance and that weaker shortcuts can escape detection.
    \item We evaluate detection up to 300,000 observations and runtime up to one million, showing that shortcut flags can coexist with relevant-feature alignment.
    \item We benchmark seven classifier families with performance confidence intervals and provide code, model artifacts, and reproduction scripts.
\end{itemize} 


\section{Related Work and Background}
% Prakrit
\label{sec:related}

Predictive maintenance studies use decision trees, random forests, and gradient-boosted trees \cite{bukhsh2019predictive}, as well as support vector methods and neural networks \cite{jalali2020interpretability}. Although small decision trees can offer interpretable rules, complex ensembles and neural networks often require additional methods to explain their predictions \cite{lundberg2017shap}. This has motivated the use of explanation methods to help maintenance teams understand model behavior. Cummins et al.~\cite{cummins2024survey} review approaches ranging from inherently interpretable models to post-hoc methods such as surrogate models, LIME, and SHAP. These methods serve different purposes: local explanations help examine individual predictions, while global summaries describe recurring patterns across observations. In maintenance applications, this distinction matters because explaining an individual alarm and understanding a model's general reliance on sensor measurements are related but different tasks.

Several studies have investigated these methods in maintenance settings. Steurtewagen and Van den Poel~\cite{steurtewagen2021adding} combine sensor and report data with an XGBoost model and use Shapley values to support the diagnosis of machinery breakdowns in the petrochemical industry. Their work connects predictive modeling with diagnostic information that can support maintenance and root-cause investigations. Jalali et al.~\cite{jalali2020interpretability} compare LIME, SHAP, and attribute-wise explanations for models trained on the PHM08-CMAPSS sensor dataset, discussing their advantages and limitations for time-series prediction. These studies illustrate both the practical use of explanations and the importance of selecting an explanation method suited to the maintenance task.

Existing predictive maintenance studies have also evaluated whether explanations agree with known failure mechanisms. Matzka~\cite{matzka2020explainable} assessed explanation quality against known failure mechanisms using selected examples, including five examples from each of four physical failure types. This provides a direct precedent for comparing explanations with the features expected to matter for a failure. Subsequent work has used SHAP to interpret predictions and feature importance on the dataset \cite{milling2025shap}. Our study extends this direction by evaluating aggregated attribution patterns on held-out observations and comparing them with a reference obtained from models retrained on shuffled labels.

SHAP assigns each input feature a contribution to an individual prediction \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Together with a baseline value, these contributions reconstruct the model output being explained. Their signs indicate the direction of their contribution, while their magnitudes indicate its size on the explained output scale. However, ``the'' Shapley value of a prediction is not unique: Shapley variants differ in how they remove a feature, for example by conditioning on the remaining features, marginalizing over a background distribution, or substituting a single baseline \cite{sundararajan2020many,covert2021explaining}. Recent surveys organize Shapley estimators by these removal choices and show that they answer different questions about the model and the data \cite{chen2023algorithms,chen2020true}, and attribution changes with the treatment of feature dependence when inputs are correlated \cite{aas2021explaining,kumar2020problems}. For tree ensembles, path-dependent TreeSHAP weights each path by its training cover, whereas interventional TreeSHAP averages over an explicit background sample \cite{lundberg2020treeshap}; faster exact algorithms such as Linear TreeSHAP \cite{yu2022linear} and Quadrature-TreeSHAP \cite{wettenstein2026quadrature} reduce the cost of the path-dependent variant but do not change what it measures. This issue is relevant when engineered features share information with the measurements from which they were constructed, and \cref{sec:variants} shows that the reference distribution can decide a mechanism's verdict. Agreement with expected trigger features must therefore be interpreted in light of the explanation formulation, rather than treated as proof of physical causation.

Broader explanation-evaluation research provides complementary ways to assess attribution quality. XAI-Bench uses synthetic data for controlled evaluation \cite{liu2021synthetic}, while OpenXAI evaluates properties including faithfulness, stability, and fairness \cite{agarwal2022openxai}. Perturbation-based approaches such as ROAR remove highly attributed inputs, retrain the model, and measure changes in predictive performance \cite{hooker2019roar}. Dependence-based global sensitivity indices, such as those built on the Hilbert--Schmidt independence criterion (HSIC) \cite{daveiga2015global}, quantify how strongly the output depends on each input without a removal step, and could serve as a model-level corroboration of attribution-based conclusions for correlated features. These studies motivate evaluating explanations beyond visual inspection or feature rankings. A complementary question is whether attributed features agree with external knowledge of the failure mechanism. Evaluating this agreement requires an external reference,
such as known failure rules, alongside evidence about the
model's attribution patterns.

Randomization offers a reference for evaluating whether an observed pattern exceeds what would arise without the original input--outcome relationship. Label-permutation tests have been used to assess classifier performance \cite{ojala2010permutation}, and outcome permutations have been used to calibrate feature-importance measures \cite{altmann2010permutation}. Adebayo et al.~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} further showed that some visually convincing saliency methods can remain insensitive to changes in model parameters or training labels. These findings motivate explicit controls when interpreting explanations. Research on shortcut learning provides another reason for such controls: models can exploit predictive associations that do not represent the intended task mechanism \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}.

Together, these studies motivate evaluating both whether explanations agree with known failure mechanisms and whether they reveal unexpected feature reliance. We address these questions through a mechanism-level audit of held-out SHAP attributions, using recorded failure types and their expected trigger features as an external reference. We assess the attribution statistics against models retrained on shuffled overall failure labels and apply correction for multiple comparisons. A controlled fabricated-sensor experiment examines the audit's ability to detect unexpected reliance and identifies cases it misses. This combines mechanism-based assessment with statistical testing and an explicit evaluation of shortcut-detection capability.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:background}

Predictive maintenance models use sensor measurements and operating
conditions to predict equipment failures before they occur. However,
a model may correctly predict a failure without relying on the physical
conditions that caused it. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is an
explainability method used to identify which features contribute to a
model's predictions. However, assigning high importance to a feature
does not necessarily mean that the model's explanation agrees with
the actual failure mechanism. This raises a key question:
\emph{When a particular failure occurs, does the model assign greater
importance to the features involved in that failure's physical rule?}

Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i,z_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote a dataset where
$x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ contains the input features, $y_i\in\{0,1\}$
indicates overall equipment failure, and $z_i\in\{0,1\}^{M}$ records
$M$ individual failure mechanisms. A classifier $f$ is trained using
only $(x_i,y_i)$; the mechanism indicators are reserved for evaluating
its explanations.

For each mechanism $m$, domain knowledge identifies a set $E_m$ of
\emph{trigger features} involved in its physical rule. We define
$S_m=\{i:z_{i,m}=1\}$ as the observations where mechanism $m$ occurs
and $F=\{i:y_i=1\}$ as all observations labeled as failures. Let
$\phi_j(x_i)$ denote the SHAP attribution of feature $j$ for observation
$i$.

Our audit evaluates three properties: (i) whether attribution is concentrated
on the expected trigger features, (ii) whether that concentration is greater
for the corresponding mechanism than for other failures, and (iii) whether
unrelated features receive unusually high attribution. These tests
measure agreement with known failure rules, not whether SHAP establishes
the physical cause of failure. \Cref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the data
flow of the audit, detailed in the remainder of this section.

\subsection{Dataset and Failure Mechanisms}
\label{sec:data}

AI4I~2020~\cite{matzka2020explainable} is a synthetic industrial
predictive maintenance dataset representing a milling process. It
contains 10{,}000 observations, each recording product quality
(\texttt{L}, \texttt{M}, or \texttt{H}), air and process temperatures,
rotational speed, torque, tool wear, an overall failure label, and
five failure-mechanism indicators. Only 339 observations (3.4\%)
are labeled as failures, making the prediction task highly imbalanced.

The dataset describes five mechanisms: tool wear failure (TWF),
heat dissipation failure (HDF), power failure (PWF), overstrain
failure (OSF), and random failure (RNF). The overall failure label
is intended to follow the logical OR of these mechanisms:

\begin{equation}
y_i^{\mathrm{OR}}
=
\mathbb{I}
\left(
\sum_{m=1}^{M}z_{i,m}\geq 1
\right),
\label{eq:failure_or}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ equals one when at least one mechanism
is active. Thus, the overall label indicates whether failure occurred,
while the mechanism indicators describe which failure conditions
were recorded. \Cref{tab:mechanisms} lists the documented rules and
the corresponding trigger features used in our audit.

\paragraph{Overlapping mechanisms and label discrepancies}
The released annotations do not always follow the intended OR
relationship. Twenty-four observations have multiple active mechanism
flags; these are valid overlapping conditions rather than labeling
errors. However, nine observations are labeled as failures without
any active flag, while 18 of the 19 RNF-flagged observations have
an overall failure label of zero.

We retain the released overall labels for training rather than
reconstructing them from the mechanism flags. The primary audit
includes every observation where the mechanism being evaluated is
active, including overlapping cases. We also repeat the analysis
using observations with exactly one active mechanism flag, removing
ambiguity about which mechanism may explain the attribution.
Comparing both analyses shows whether overlapping conditions affect
the conclusions. RNF has no physical trigger features and is
examined only descriptively.

\subsection{Feature Construction}
\label{sec:features}

Several mechanisms depend on combinations of sensor readings rather
than individual measurements. We therefore construct four features
from the original inputs: mechanical power
$P=\tau(2\pi n/60)$, temperature difference
$\Delta T=T_{\mathrm{process}}-T_{\mathrm{air}}$,
overstrain $O=w\tau$, and overstrain ratio $Q=O/L(t)$,
where $\tau$, $n$, and $w$ denote torque, rotational speed,
and tool wear, respectively. The type-specific limit $L(t)$
is 11{,}000, 12{,}000, or 13{,}000 for product types
\texttt{L}, \texttt{M}, and \texttt{H}.

These features explicitly represent the interactions used in the
HDF, PWF, and OSF rules. They are calculated from process measurements
and product type, without using failure labels or mechanism flags.
However, they are correlated with the original measurements and
encode known parts of the failure rules. We therefore repeat the
audit using only the original features to determine how much the
results depend on feature construction (\cref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Explanation Audit Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

We evaluate attribution using three complementary statistics.
For feature $j$ and observation set $A$, let

\begin{equation}
I_{j}(A)
=
\frac{1}{|A|}
\sum_{i\in A}|\phi_j(x_i)|,
\qquad
s_j(A)
=
\frac{I_{j}(A)}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}I_{k}(A)}
\label{eq:mean_shap}
\end{equation}

denote the average absolute SHAP contribution and the attribution
share of feature $j$, where $\phi_j(x_i)$ is computed by the
cross-fitted model that did not train on observation $i$
(\cref{sec:oos}). We use absolute values because positive and
negative contributions could otherwise cancel. The fraction of
attribution assigned to the expected trigger features of
mechanism $m$ is

\begin{equation}
r_m(A)
=
\sum_{j\in E_m}s_j(A).
\label{eq:relevant_fraction}
\end{equation}

From these quantities, we define three statistics:

\begin{align}
R_m &= r_m(S_m),
\label{eq:share}\\
D_m &= r_m(S_m)-r_m(F\setminus S_m),
\label{eq:spec}\\
C_m &= \max_{j\notin E_m}
\left[
s_j(S_m)-s_j(F\setminus S_m)
\right],
\label{eq:shortcut}
\end{align}

All three are computed on the same matrix of held-out absolute
SHAP values; only the observation sets and feature sets differ.

The \emph{relevant score} $R_m$ measures how much attribution
goes to the expected trigger features on mechanism $m$'s
observations. The \emph{specificity score} $D_m$ asks whether
those features receive a larger share for this mechanism than
for other labeled failures. The \emph{shortcut score} $C_m$
measures the largest mechanism-specific increase in attribution
among features outside the expected trigger set. A large $C_m$
raises concern about reliance on another feature, although it
does not prove that the feature is causally irrelevant.

For example, among the 95 PWF observations, the average absolute
SHAP contributions of power, torque, and rotational speed are
4.96, 1.93, and 1.33, respectively (\cref{fig:shap}a).
Their sum is 8.22, compared with 11.82 across all twelve
inputs, giving $R_{\mathrm{PWF}}=8.22/11.82\approx0.70$.
Thus, approximately 70\% of the attribution on PWF observations
goes to features involved in its physical rule. Whether this
agreement exceeds what could occur by chance is determined
through statistical testing.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Explanations}
\label{sec:oos}

Explanations calculated on training observations may reflect
patterns the model has fitted particularly closely to those
observations. We therefore use five-fold cross-fitting
~\cite{chernozhukov2018double}. The data are divided into
five stratified folds. In each round, the complete training
pipeline is fitted on four folds, and SHAP values are calculated
on the held-out fold. After five rounds, every observation has
an explanation from a model that was not trained on it.
We combine these held-out explanations for the audit and
also examine the effect of using in-sample SHAP values
(\cref{sec:robust}).

\subsection{Statistical Significance}
\label{sec:nulls}

A high attribution score does not necessarily indicate meaningful
alignment. Mechanism-specific observations often contain extreme
sensor values, which can receive large SHAP contributions even
when the model has learned no genuine relationship with failure.
This makes a simple comparison against randomly selected failure
observations unreliable (\cref{sec:naive}).

We instead construct a \emph{shuffled-label null}. For each null
model, we randomly permute the overall failure labels while
keeping the sensor measurements and mechanism indicators unchanged.
This preserves the input distribution and failure rate but removes
the original relationship between inputs and failure outcomes.
We repeat the full preprocessing, oversampling, training,
cross-fitting, and explanation procedure, using the hyperparameters
selected on the original data. Because carrying these hyperparameters
over could itself shape the null, we also rebuild it with the full
hyperparameter search repeated on every permutation (\cref{sec:robust}).

Repeating this process $K$ times provides a reference distribution
for each statistic. For an observed value $T$ and null values
$T^{(1)},\ldots,T^{(K)}$, we calculate the upper-tail
Monte Carlo $p$-value:

\begin{equation}
p=
\frac{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\mathbb{I}(T^{(k)}\geq T)}
{K+1}.
\label{eq:pvalue}
\end{equation}

The primary audit uses $K=1{,}000$, giving a minimum possible
$p$-value of $1/1001\approx0.001$. We apply Holm's
correction~\cite{holm1979simple} across the four physical
mechanisms for each statistic and use $\alpha=0.05$.
To complement the $p$-values, we report a null-standardized effect
size $z=(T-\bar{T}_{0})/s_{0}$, where $\bar{T}_{0}$ and $s_{0}$ are
the mean and standard deviation of the null values, and 95\%
percentile bootstrap intervals for $R_m$ and $D_m$ obtained by
resampling observations within $S_m$ and $F\setminus S_m$
(5{,}000 resamples). The bootstrap holds the cross-fitted models
fixed, so it reflects observation sampling only; variability from
refitting is captured by the null.

We combine the three tests into one decision rule. A mechanism
is \emph{aligned} when both $R_m$ and $D_m$ are significantly
larger than their null distributions and $C_m$ is not; it is
\emph{flagged} when $C_m$ is significant, whatever $R_m$ and
$D_m$ show, and \emph{not aligned} otherwise. The shortcut test
can therefore overturn a positive verdict, which matters at
large $n$, where a narrow null lets $R_m$ pass even when a
non-trigger feature takes much of the attribution
(\cref{sec:scale}). A flag names the responsible feature and its
excess share, which should be inspected before acting on it.
A non-significant shortcut result means that the audit did not
detect such an increase; it does not establish that shortcuts
are absent.
RNF is excluded from the physical-alignment tests because
it has no expected trigger features.

\subsection{Model Training and Evaluation}
\label{sec:training}

We split the dataset into stratified training, validation, and
test sets (70/20/10). Numerical scaling and one-hot encoding
are fitted on training data only. Because failures account
for just 3.4\% of observations, we compare SMOTE
~\cite{chawla2002smote}, Borderline-SMOTE
~\cite{han2005borderline}, and ADASYN~\cite{he2008adasyn}
using the validation F1 of a default XGBoost model
~\cite{chen2016xgboost}. Borderline-SMOTE gives the
highest validation F1 and is used thereafter.

We compare seven classifiers: logistic regression, an RBF-kernel
SVM, $k$-NN ($k=7$), a decision tree (depth 10), a random
forest (300 trees, depth 12), XGBoost (300 trees, depth 6,
learning rate 0.1), and an MLP with hidden layers of 64
and 32 units. Other hyperparameters use library defaults
(scikit-learn 1.9.1, XGBoost 3.4.1, and PyTorch 2.14.0),
with random seed 42.

XGBoost achieves the highest validation F1. We tune it using
40 iterations of randomized search with five-fold stratified
cross-validation, optimizing F1. The search covers tree depth
3--8, 200--600 trees, learning rate 0.01--0.2, row and
column subsampling 0.6--1.0, minimum child weight 1--5,
and $\gamma$ 0--0.3. Borderline-SMOTE is applied within
each training fold to avoid oversampling leakage. We select
the failure-probability threshold that maximizes validation F1.

We evaluate predictive performance using failure-class precision,
recall, F1, ROC-AUC, Brier score, and expected calibration error.
We report 95\% confidence intervals using exact binomial
(Clopper--Pearson) intervals for precision and recall and
5{,}000 stratified bootstrap resamples for other metrics.
We additionally report five-fold cross-validation results at
threshold 0.5. Because this evaluation reuses data involved
in hyperparameter selection, its estimates may be slightly
optimistic.

\paragraph{Robustness checks}
We repeat the audit across four additional random seeds, on
single-mechanism observations, without engineered features,
and using in-sample rather than held-out SHAP values. We also
compare path-dependent TreeSHAP with interventional TreeSHAP,
using background samples of 100, 500, and 1{,}000 training
observations per fold, drawn uniformly at random from that
fold's original (not oversampled) training observations with
up to three background seeds, and repeat the audit with a random
forest. To examine the TWF result, we further draw the interventional
background only from non-failures, only from failures, or from the
oversampled training data, and run the interventional audit on the
original features alone. Finally, to test whether the audit depends
on tree ensembles or TreeSHAP, we audit the MLP of \cref{sec:training}
with two non-tree explainers: KernelSHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, a
model-agnostic Shapley estimator (25 training observations as
background, 1{,}000 coalitions per observation), and Integrated
Gradients~\cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}, a gradient-based method
(baseline equal to the mean of 100 training observations, 1{,}024
steps, completeness checked to within 5\%). Both explain the MLP's
logit, and every null model retrains the MLP on permuted labels and
is explained in the same way. Each alternative uses a matching shuffled-label
null procedure.

\paragraph{Controlled shortcut experiment}
To test whether the audit detects reliance on an unrelated
but predictive feature, we introduce an artificial sensor

\begin{equation}
s_i=z_{i,\mathrm{HDF}}+\epsilon_i,
\qquad
\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).
\label{eq:shortcut_sensor}
\end{equation}

This sensor is a noisy copy of the HDF indicator, created
only for this controlled experiment. It has no physical role
in heat dissipation failure and is excluded from
$E_{\mathrm{HDF}}$. We decrease $\sigma$ from 2 to 0.1;
smaller values make the artificial sensor more predictive.
We examine whether the audit detects increasing reliance
on this shortcut.

\paragraph{Scaling experiment}
We generate new observations using the documented AI4I~2020
process~\cite{matzka2020explainable}, without copying released
rows. The sensor distributions follow the documented process,
and HDF, PWF, and OSF use their published rules. Tool wear
increases until a lifetime drawn from $U(200,240)$\,min,
where TWF occurs with probability 0.4. The generated failure
rate is 3.5--4.0\%, compared with 3.4\% in the release.

We evaluate $n\in\{10^4,3\cdot10^4,10^5,3\cdot10^5\}$
with three generation seeds for the first three sizes and
one for the largest. Each setting is tested without a
shortcut and with shortcut noise
$\sigma\in\{0.35,0.25\}$. The scaling experiments use
$K=200$ null models, and the generator is included in
the released code.

\paragraph{Why simulated data}
We use AI4I~2020 because its documented failure mechanisms
provide a reference for evaluating explanation alignment,
which is rarely available in real maintenance datasets.
Although the data are generated from known rules, the
classifier is trained only on overall failure labels and
may learn alternative predictive relationships. Agreement
with the physical rules is therefore not automatic.

The shuffled-label, shortcut, and raw-feature experiments
test whether the audit can distinguish mechanism-consistent
attribution from chance agreement or reliance on alternative
features. Nevertheless, the findings establish agreement
with known simulated failure rules, not causal validity
in real industrial systems.

the default XGBoost. Its validation-selected decision threshold
is 0.978. However, the confidence intervals overlap, so this
difference does not establish a reliable improvement from tuning.

\paragraph{HDF, PWF, and OSF}

\subsection{Robustness of Mechanism Alignment}
\label{sec:robust}

\paragraph{Random seeds and overlapping mechanisms}
HDF, PWF, and OSF pass both alignment tests across all
five random seeds and when the analysis is restricted
to observations with a single active mechanism.
TWF fails both tests across the five seeds.
This suggests that the main findings are not driven
by a particular random seed or by overlapping
mechanism indicators.

\paragraph{Re-tuned null}
Repeating the 40-iteration hyperparameter search on each of
200 permutations gives a wider null than carrying over the
hyperparameters selected on the real labels: the search on
shuffled labels mostly selects shallow trees (depth at most 4
in 115 of 200 permutations), and the null 95th percentile of
$R_{\mathrm{OSF}}$ rises from 0.46 to 0.62. The verdicts are
unchanged. HDF, PWF, and OSF pass both tests and TWF fails
both ($p_R=0.453$, $p_D=0.368$), but the OSF relevant-share
margin narrows: 4 of 200 re-tuned null models reach the
observed 0.672, giving an adjusted $p=0.0498$. The carried-over
null is therefore the less conservative reference, and the
OSF relevant-share result should be read as borderline under
the stricter one.

\paragraph{In-sample versus out-of-sample explanations}
When explanations are calculated on observations used
for training, all four mechanisms pass, including TWF
($p=0.004$). Its relevant score increases from 0.19
with out-of-sample SHAP to 0.35 with in-sample SHAP.
This difference shows that evaluating training
observations can produce a more favorable alignment
assessment. We therefore use cross-fitting for
the primary audit.

\paragraph{Original features only}
Removing the engineered features changes the verdict for HDF, which retains significant specificity but no longer passes the relevant-share test. PWF and OSF continue to pass both tests, although OSF specificity is close to the threshold ($p=0.048$). TWF still fails. Thus, alignment for PWF and OSF is not dependent on explicitly providing the derived features, whereas the HDF result is sensitive to feature construction. This is consistent with the difficulty of representing a temperature-difference rule using only separate temperature measurements in a tree model.

\subsection{Why the Choice of Null Matters}
\label{sec:naive}

A statistical comparison should reject a model that has learned no genuine relationship between sensor measurements and failures. Our shuffled-label null satisfies this check: a classifier trained on shuffled failure labels fails the alignment tests. However, when we compare that same classifier against randomly selected failure observations under the same model, all four mechanisms appear significant (adjusted $p=0.002$). The naive comparison therefore approves a model trained on meaningless labels. The likely explanation is that mechanism observations contain unusual sensor values that can receive high SHAP attribution even without meaningful failure learning. This result supports using retrained shuffled-label models rather than relying on differences between observation groups alone.

\subsection{Can the Audit Detect a Shortcut?}
\label{sec:sweep}

We introduce a synthetic feature that is correlated with the HDF indicator but has no physical role in heat dissipation failure. By reducing its noise, we make this feature increasingly useful for predicting HDF. \Cref{fig:sweep} shows how attribution shifts from the documented trigger features to the artificial sensor.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/shortcut_sweep.pdf}
\caption{Effect of an injected shortcut on HDF attribution.
As noise decreases, the artificial sensor receives a
larger attribution share while the expected trigger
features receive less. Markers indicate settings where
the relevant-share alignment test fails.}
\label{fig:sweep}
\end{figure}

The audit responds differently depending on
the strength of the shortcut.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Weak shortcut ($\sigma\geq0.4$):}
The artificial sensor receives at most 8\% of
attribution, and HDF continues to pass.

\item \textbf{Moderate shortcut
($\sigma=0.35$--$0.30$):}
At 13\% shortcut attribution, the relevant-share
test still passes ($p=0.044$). When the share
reaches 21\%, this test fails.

\item \textbf{Strong shortcut ($\sigma\leq0.25$):}
At approximately 35\% shortcut attribution,
the shortcut statistic also becomes significant.
The specificity test fails once the artificial
sensor receives approximately 42\%.
\end{itemize}

Throughout the experiment, test F1 remains between
0.84 and 0.92. Thus, the model can maintain good
predictive performance while increasingly relying
on a feature unrelated to the documented mechanism.

PWF and OSF continue to pass across the shortcut
settings, while the injected feature affects HDF.
However, the audit does not reliably detect weak
shortcuts, and the shortcut statistic is less
sensitive than the relevant-share test at moderate
strengths.

\subsection{Effect of the Model and SHAP Method}
\label{sec:variants}

HDF, PWF, and OSF pass the alignment tests using both path-dependent and interventional TreeSHAP, and their verdicts remain unchanged with a random forest.

\paragraph{Beyond trees}
The audit itself needs only an attribution matrix, so it runs unchanged on the MLP (test F1 0.76) with KernelSHAP and Integrated Gradients, each against 200 retrained MLP nulls (\cref{tab:robust}). With Integrated Gradients, HDF and OSF are aligned (adjusted $p\leq0.030$ for both tests), PWF passes the relevant-share test narrowly ($p=0.0498$) but not specificity ($p=0.10$), and TWF fails. With KernelSHAP, no mechanism is aligned; only PWF specificity is significant. Two factors make these verdicts weaker than for XGBoost. First, shuffled-label MLPs already place much attribution on the trigger features, so the null means are high (e.g., $R_{\mathrm{PWF}}$ null mean 0.60--0.63 versus 0.35 for XGBoost), likely because a smooth network trained on noise still spreads attribution over the inputs that the PWF and OSF rules use. Second, the MLP is a weaker classifier, and KernelSHAP adds sampling error from its 25-row background and 1{,}000 of the 4{,}094 coalitions. We cannot separate these factors here, but the result shows why the shuffled-label null is needed: under KernelSHAP, the raw relevant shares of HDF, PWF, and OSF (0.47--0.67) look like alignment, yet none exceeds what an MLP without failure knowledge produces.

TWF behaves differently. It passes both tests with interventional TreeSHAP across the tested background sizes and seeds, with relevant scores between 0.45 and 0.47. However, it fails with path-dependent TreeSHAP and the random forest. Tool wear is strongly correlated with the engineered overstrain features overall (Spearman 0.92), and different SHAP formulations distribute attribution among dependent features differently~\cite{aas2021explaining}. Correlation alone does not explain the flip, however: on TWF observations the correlation is only 0.13, and path-dependent TWF alignment also fails without the engineered features (\cref{tab:robust}). Interventional TreeSHAP also passes TWF on the raw features alone ($R=0.58$, $p=0.020$).

The flip is better explained by the reference distribution against which each formulation measures a feature's contribution. Path-dependent TreeSHAP weights tree paths by the training cover, which reflects the oversampled data the model was fitted on, whereas our interventional background is drawn from the original training observations, about 97\% of which are non-failures. We therefore repeat the interventional audit with three backgrounds (\cref{tab:robust}), each with a matching null. A non-failure background reproduces the default result ($R_{\mathrm{TWF}}=0.46$). A background drawn from the oversampled training data lowers $R_{\mathrm{TWF}}$ to 0.23, close to the path-dependent 0.19, although TWF still passes narrowly ($p_R=0.045$). A failure-only background lowers it to 0.08 and also breaks HDF alignment. The attribution therefore answers different questions under different references: against typical operation, tool wear is what distinguishes TWF observations, but against other failures it is not. Thus, the TWF verdict depends mainly on the choice of reference distribution rather than on feature correlation, and remains unresolved in the sense that no single choice is correct for all purposes. For maintenance audits that ask why an observation is abnormal, we recommend a background drawn from normal operation and reporting it alongside the result.

\subsection{Does the Audit Work with More Data?}
\label{sec:scale}

We repeat the audit on generated datasets containing $10^4$ to $3\cdot10^5$ observations. Without an injected shortcut, HDF, PWF, and OSF pass the alignment tests at every evaluated size and seed. TWF continues to fail the relevant-share test, showing that additional observations alone do not resolve its alignment.

The shortcut experiments reveal a different pattern. At $\sigma=0.25$, the artificial sensor receives 27--45\% of HDF attribution. The relevant-share test detects the resulting loss of alignment in all nine runs up to 100{,}000 observations, but not at 300{,}000. At the largest size, the observed relevant score is 0.472, exceeding the null's 95th percentile of 0.417. As the null distribution becomes narrower, a model can remain significantly better than shuffled-label models while relying heavily on a misleading feature.

The shortcut score detects the stronger shortcut in all ten evaluated runs, including at 300{,}000 observations. At that size, its observed value is 0.249, compared with the null's 95th percentile of 0.211. Under the combined decision rule, HDF is therefore flagged rather than aligned in that run; across all experiments, this is the only case in which the shortcut test changes a verdict that $R_m$ and $D_m$ alone would have given. The weaker shortcut at $\sigma=0.35$, which receives 6--20\% of attribution, is not detected reliably.

The shortcut score also flags TWF in 7 of 30 runs from $3\cdot10^4$ observations onward, three of them without an injected sensor, always pointing to air or process temperature with small excess shares (0.03--0.05). The generator draws temperatures independently of tool life, so these flags cannot reflect a genuine shortcut. They likely arise because attribution on TWF observations, which the model captures poorly, is spread more evenly across features than on other failures, and the narrowing null at large $n$ makes this small excess significant. A significant $C_m$ should therefore be read together with the size of the excess share and the identity of the flagged feature.

\paragraph{Computational cost}
The audit requires $5(K+1)$ model fits because each real and randomized model is evaluated through five-fold cross-fitting. A single cross-fitted run takes approximately 1.3 seconds for $10^4$ observations, 31 seconds for $3\cdot10^5$, and 115 seconds for $10^6$ observations on one core, growing roughly linearly with $n$. With eight cores, the complete $K=200$ audit takes about three minutes at $10^4$ observations and approximately one hour at $3\cdot10^5$. The independent randomized runs can be distributed across multiple processors or machines.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study evaluated whether SHAP explanations of predictive
maintenance models align with known physical failure mechanisms.
Using AI4I~2020, we proposed a statistical audit that compares
feature attributions with expected failure conditions and
shuffled-label models. Despite achieving a test F1 of 0.89,
the XGBoost model showed consistent alignment for three failure
mechanisms, while tool wear results depended on the reference
distribution used by the explanation method, and a neural network
showed weaker, explainer-dependent alignment. The audit also revealed that accurate predictions can
rely on misleading features and that simpler evaluation methods
may fail to detect them. These findings highlight the need to
evaluate explanations beyond predictive accuracy to better
assess their agreement with known failure mechanisms.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits_future}

\paragraph{Simulated data}
The audit is evaluated on the simulated AI4I~2020 data and on data
generated from the same documented process, so it establishes
agreement with known simulator rules, not verified physical causes
in real systems. We could not address this within the present study,
because real maintenance datasets rarely record which mechanism caused
each failure. The audit also requires predefined trigger features,
which domain experts must supply for real equipment.

\paragraph{Reference distribution and explainer}
We traced the TWF disagreement between SHAP formulations mainly to the
reference distribution (\cref{sec:variants}), but this does not
resolve which reference is correct: verdicts can change with the
background, and an oversampled background still leaves a small gap
to path-dependent TreeSHAP. Results on the MLP depend on the explainer,
and we could not separate the MLP's weaker fit from KernelSHAP's
sampling error. LIME was not evaluated, because its surrogate
coefficients are not an additive decomposition of the prediction,
which the attribution shares assume.

\paragraph{Shortcut detection}
The shortcut statistic first flags the planted sensor at 35\% of the
HDF attribution, whereas the relevant-share test already fails at
21\%, and no test detects a sensor taking 13\% or less. A studentized variant, which scales each feature's
excess by its own null spread, was less sensitive still (first flag
at 42\% rather than 35\% shortcut attribution), because the planted
sensor already takes a large and variable excess share under the null
(mean 0.087, s.d.\ 0.060). At large $n$, the statistic also flags
small excesses (0.03--0.05) on poorly learned mechanisms, so flags
must be read with their size and feature.

\paragraph{Statistical scope}
Re-tuning the null models widens the null and leaves OSF's relevant
share borderline ($p=0.0498$); re-tuning costs about 50 minutes for
$K=200$, so the primary audit uses carried-over hyperparameters. The
bootstrap intervals hold the models fixed. TWF, with 46 observations
and a random failure point, and RNF remain hard to assess.

\paragraph{Future work}
We plan to apply the audit to real data with recorded failure causes,
including overlapping faults in the Tennessee Eastman
Process~\cite{rieth2017tepdata,rieth2018issues}; to develop shortcut
statistics that are more sensitive without more false flags; and to
add complementary statistics that use the whole attribution
distribution, such as rank-based or earth mover's distances to the
expected feature set, together with dependence-based global
sensitivity indices~\cite{daveiga2015global} as an attribution-free
check for correlated features.
