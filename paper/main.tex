\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models are widely used to predict equipment failures in industrial systems, but accurate predictions do not guarantee that models rely on features consistent with physical failure mechanisms. Although explainable artificial intelligence methods identify influential features, whether these explanations align with documented failure mechanisms is rarely tested statistically. To address this gap, we propose a mechanism-level statistical audit that evaluates whether feature attributions concentrate on mechanism-relevant features, distinguish among failure mechanisms, and reveal potentially misleading feature reliance. Three complementary statistics are evaluated against reference distributions generated by retraining models on shuffled labels. Experiments on the AI4I 2020 benchmark show evidence of attribution alignment for three of four documented failure mechanisms. Additional evaluations on laboratory bearing recordings and simulated industrial process data demonstrate the applicability of the framework across different settings. Controlled shortcut experiments show that models can maintain high predictive performance while relying on misleading features, although weaker or distributed shortcuts may not be detected. Audit conclusions are also sensitive to the choice of explanation method and reference distribution. Overall, the proposed framework enables systematic statistical evaluation of explanation–mechanism alignment, while recognizing that attribution agreement does not establish physical correctness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% Prakrit

% Context and importance
Predictive maintenance is a maintenance strategy that uses machine-condition data to help anticipate faults and determine when maintenance should be performed. By identifying developing problems, it can help reduce unplanned downtime \cite{cummins2024survey}. Machine-learning models support this process by analyzing equipment data to predict failures and maintenance needs. These models include decision trees, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, support vector methods, and neural networks \cite{bukhsh2019predictive,jalali2020interpretability}. Complex ensembles and neural networks can be difficult to interpret, making prediction performance alone insufficient for understanding their behavior \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Understanding which sensor readings influence their predictions can help maintenance teams assess an alarm and decide what to investigate \cite{steurtewagen2021adding}. Interpretability approaches used in predictive maintenance include decision trees and local surrogate explanations such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) \cite{bukhsh2019predictive}, as well as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for interpreting sensor contributions to model predictions \cite{steurtewagen2021adding,gawde2024explainable}. Recent work has also examined how preprocessing and model complexity affect explanation quality in remaining-useful-life prediction \cite{ndao2025improving}.

% Specific problem and evaluation challenge
High predictive performance can coexist with reliance on shortcuts or correlations unrelated to the intended task \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}. In predictive maintenance, this raises the question of whether explanations emphasize the expected features for each failure type. SHAP assigns feature contributions to individual predictions \cite{lundberg2017shap}, but these contributions do not by themselves establish the physical relevance of those inputs \cite{janzing2020feature}. Evaluating feature attributions is difficult when a suitable ground-truth reference is unavailable \cite{zhou2022attribute}. Assessing mechanism alignment therefore requires information about which failure mechanism occurred and which features are expected to matter. Label-permutation methods provide a statistical reference for evaluating feature importance when the association between inputs and outcomes is disrupted \cite{altmann2010permutation}. The question is therefore not only which features receive attribution, but whether their attribution agrees with the recorded mechanism and exceeds that observed under this reference.

% Evaluation settings, prior work, and research gap
In this work, we investigate this question using the AI4I 2020 predictive maintenance dataset, a synthetic dataset containing machine operating measurements, an overall failure label, and separate failure-type labels \cite{uci2020ai4i}. Its recorded failure types and documented generation rules provide an external reference for evaluating mechanism alignment. Previous work on this dataset has assessed explanation quality using selected examples \cite{matzka2020explainable}, interpreted SHAP feature importance \cite{milling2025shap}, or quantitatively compared SHAP and LIME rankings \cite{obeten2026agreement}. Selected examples provide limited evidence across a failure type, while feature rankings and agreement between explainers do not by themselves establish agreement with its failure rule. This motivates our statistical audit of mechanism-level attribution on held-out observations, using models retrained on shuffled overall failure labels as a reference \cite{altmann2010permutation}.

% Overview of the proposed approach
For the primary AI4I audit, we train a classifier to predict overall machine failure while withholding the individual failure-type labels from its inputs. We use SHAP to explain individual model predictions. Its feature contributions let us assess whether explanations emphasize the features specified by each failure rule. We compute SHAP values for tree ensembles using TreeSHAP \cite{lundberg2020treeshap}. To assess robustness beyond tree models, we audit a multilayer perceptron (MLP) using KernelSHAP and DeepSHAP \cite{lundberg2017shap}. We group the SHAP explanations of individual model predictions by failure mechanism using the recorded failure-type labels. The audit assesses mechanism alignment by comparing held-out SHAP attributions with features identified as relevant by documented failure rules. Three statistics measure the attribution share on these features, how much larger that share is than on other failure types, and the largest excess share on a feature outside that set relative to other failures. Models retrained on shuffled overall failure labels provide a reference for assessing these patterns after disrupting the relationship between model inputs and failure outcomes. We first evaluate the audit on AI4I, then apply it to Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) laboratory bearing recordings \cite{cwru_data} and simulated Tennessee Eastman process (TEP) data \cite{reinartz2021tep} to examine its applicability across different datasets and failure mechanisms. Supporting experiments assess sensitivity to models, explainers, and attribution references, and test the detection of planted shortcuts.

% Contributions build on the approach, progressing from method to evaluation and findings.
The paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a statistical framework for auditing whether model explanations align with documented failure mechanisms. It combines three mechanism-level attribution statistics, held-out explanations, a reference from models retrained on shuffled overall failure labels, and multiple-comparison correction.

    \item We investigate how alignment conclusions depend on statistical references, models, explainers, and feature definitions. Controlled shortcut experiments establish the audit's ability and limits in detecting misleading reliance that predictive performance alone can conceal.

    \item We demonstrate the framework's applicability across AI4I synthetic maintenance data, CWRU laboratory bearing recordings, and Tennessee Eastman process simulations. We assess robustness and scalability, and provide predictive benchmarks and artifacts for reproducing the experiments.

\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
% Prakrit
\label{sec:related}

% Explainable predictive maintenance and local versus global interpretation
Explainable AI methods, including LIME and SHAP, have been studied in predictive maintenance to help interpret machine-learning predictions \cite{cummins2024survey,steurtewagen2021adding,gawde2024explainable}. Although small decision trees can provide interpretable rules, ensembles and neural networks often require additional explanation methods. Local explanations help examine individual alarms, while global summaries describe recurring patterns of feature importance. These provide different levels of insight into model behavior.

% Applications in machinery maintenance and process diagnosis
Maintenance studies apply explanation methods to both prognostics and fault diagnosis. Steurtewagen and Van den Poel~\cite{steurtewagen2021adding} combine sensor and report data with XGBoost and use Shapley values to investigate machinery breakdowns in the petrochemical industry. Jalali et al.~\cite{jalali2020interpretability} compare LIME, SHAP, and attribute-wise explanations for models predicting the remaining useful life of aircraft engines using PHM08-CMAPSS sensor data. Gawde et al.~\cite{gawde2024explainable} apply several explanation methods to rotating-machine fault diagnosis. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025csshap} extend SHAP to the cyclic-spectral domain, relating carrier and modulation frequency contributions to bearing fault characteristics on CWRU data. In process monitoring, Gravanis et al.~\cite{gravanis2026explainability} compare Integrated Gradients and SHAP explanations of an LSTM classifier on TEP and examine their consistency with process knowledge. Together, these studies illustrate how explanations support prognostics and fault diagnosis, including assessment against domain knowledge.

% Existing mechanism-based assessments on AI4I
On AI4I 2020, existing studies examine explanations from several perspectives. Matzka~\cite{matzka2020explainable} assesses explanations against the dataset's failure rules using selected examples. Zhao et al.~\cite{milling2025shap} examine SHAP feature importance, while Obeten and Jimoh~\cite{obeten2026agreement} quantitatively compare SHAP and LIME rankings. Together, these studies assess physical plausibility, feature importance, and consistency between explanation methods.

% Explanation definitions, feature dependence, and computational advances
The interpretation of feature attributions also depends on the assumptions made by explanation methods. SHAP assigns additive feature contributions relative to a baseline \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Covert et al.~\cite{covert2021explaining} explain that removal-based methods differ in how they represent features treated as missing when evaluating model predictions. Their framework highlights that assumptions about unavailable features are part of the explanation definition. Path-dependent TreeSHAP uses training-data coverage along tree paths, whereas interventional TreeSHAP averages over an explicit background dataset \cite{lundberg2020treeshap}. Quadrature-TreeSHAP accelerates computation while retaining the path-dependent formulation \cite{wettenstein2026quadrature}. Faster computation therefore addresses a different concern from choosing the attribution reference. Feature dependence can also change how attribution is distributed, particularly when engineered inputs share information with their source measurements \cite{chen2020true,aas2021explaining}. For neural models, Integrated Gradients accumulates gradients along a path from a baseline to the explained observation \cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic}, and baseline selection affects the resulting explanation \cite{sturmfels2020visualizing}.

% Evaluation frameworks and complementary sensitivity analysis
Broader explanation-evaluation research examines several properties of attribution quality. XAI-Bench uses synthetic data for controlled evaluation \cite{liu2021synthetic}, while OpenXAI evaluates properties including faithfulness, stability, and fairness \cite{agarwal2022openxai}. ROAR removes highly attributed inputs, retrains the model, and measures changes in predictive performance \cite{hooker2019roar}. Global sensitivity analysis based on the Hilbert--Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) measures statistical dependence between inputs and outputs \cite{daveiga2015global}. Applied to model predictions, HSIC characterizes dependence between input features and predicted outputs.

% Statistical references and shortcut learning
Statistical tests assess predictive performance and feature importance, while randomization checks examine how explanations respond to shuffled training labels or randomized model parameters. Label-permutation tests have been used to assess classifier performance \cite{ojala2010permutation}, and outcome permutations have been used to calibrate feature-importance measures \cite{altmann2010permutation}. Adebayo et al.~\cite{adebayo2018sanity} showed that some visually convincing saliency methods remain insensitive to changes in model parameters or training labels. Recent work by Kim et al.~\cite{kim2025phitest} supplements global SHAP rankings for regression models with statistical summaries of direction, uncertainty, fidelity, and stability. Beyond these statistical assessments, shortcut-learning research shows that accurate predictions can rely on unintended cues, motivating closer examination of which features models use \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}.

% How the present study builds on prior work
Existing research provides complementary ways to interpret predictions and evaluate feature attributions. However, feature rankings, agreement between explainers, and faithfulness to model behavior do not by themselves establish alignment with documented failure mechanisms. Our work complements these approaches through a statistical audit of mechanism-level alignment and attribution to features outside those identified as relevant by documented failure rules.



\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:background}

Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i,z_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ contains the input features, $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicates overall failure, and $z_i\in\{0,1\}^{M}$ records $M$ failure mechanisms. A classifier $f$ is trained using only $(x_i,y_i)$; the mechanism indicators are reserved for evaluating its explanations. For each mechanism $m$, domain knowledge defines a set $E_m$ of expected features. Let $S_m=\{i:z_{i,m}=1\}$ denote mechanism-positive observations, $F=\{i:y_i=1\}$ all labeled failures, and $\phi_j(x_i)$ the SHAP attribution of feature $j$ for observation $i$.

Our goal is to test whether explanations emphasize the expected features when a particular failure mechanism occurs, distinguish that mechanism from other failures, and reveal unusual reliance on features outside the expected set. The audit measures agreement with a predefined mechanism description, not whether the model has discovered the physical cause of failure.


\subsection{Overview of the Proposed Audit}



\Cref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the four stages of the proposed audit. First, a classifier is trained using sensor features $x$ and overall failure labels $y$, while the mechanism indicators $z$ and predefined expected feature sets $E_m$ are reserved for evaluating its explanations. Second, cross-fitting generates SHAP values for observations not used to train the corresponding model. Third, the explanations are grouped into observations associated with mechanism $m$ ($S_m$) and other failure observations ($F\setminus S_m$). The audit then calculates three statistics: $R_m$ measures attribution to expected features, $D_m$ compares this attribution between the two groups, and $C_m$ measures excess attribution to features outside $E_m$.

In the final stage, the overall failure labels are shuffled while the sensor features and mechanism indicators remain unchanged. The complete training and explanation pipeline is repeated $K$ times to obtain reference distributions for the three statistics. The observed values are compared with these distributions, and the resulting $p$-values are adjusted using Holm's correction. A significant $C_m$ produces a flagged verdict; otherwise, a mechanism is considered aligned when both $R_m$ and $D_m$ are significant, and not aligned when these conditions are not met. This procedure tests whether the observed explanation pattern provides stronger evidence of mechanism agreement than the shuffled-label reference. The mathematical definitions and decision rules are provided in the following subsections.

\subsection{Audit Statistics}
\label{sec:stats}

We use absolute SHAP values to measure how strongly each feature contributes to the model's predictions, regardless of whether the contribution increases or decreases the predicted failure score. For feature $j$ and observation set $A$, we first calculate its mean absolute attribution and then normalize it by the total attribution across all features:

\begin{equation}
I_j(A)=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{i\in A}|\phi_j(x_i)|,
\qquad
s_j(A)=\frac{I_j(A)}{\sum_{k=1}^{d}I_k(A)}.
\label{eq:mean_shap}
\end{equation}

Here, $I_j(A)$ measures the average attribution of feature $j$, while $s_j(A)$ represents its share of the total attribution. For mechanism $m$, let $r_m(A)=\sum_{j\in E_m}s_j(A)$ denote the combined share assigned to its expected features. Using these quantities, we define three complementary statistics:

\begin{align}
R_m &= r_m(S_m),
\label{eq:share}\\
D_m &= r_m(S_m)-r_m(F\setminus S_m),
\label{eq:spec}\\
C_m &= \max_{j\notin E_m}
\left[s_j(S_m)-s_j(F\setminus S_m)\right].
\label{eq:shortcut}
\end{align}

The \emph{relevant share} $R_m$ measures how much attribution is assigned to the expected features when mechanism $m$ occurs. However, these features may also be important for other failures, so a large $R_m$ alone does not establish mechanism-specific alignment. The \emph{differential score} $D_m$ addresses this by comparing the expected-feature share on mechanism-positive observations with that on other failures. Finally, the \emph{outside-set excess} $C_m$ measures the largest increase in attribution share among features outside $E_m$, allowing the audit to identify unexpected feature reliance that the first two statistics may overlook.

Together, the statistics examine whether explanations emphasize the expected features, distinguish the mechanism from other failures, and reveal unexpected attribution. Their statistical significance is determined using the shuffled-label reference described in \cref{sec:nulls}. A significant outside-set excess requires further investigation because it may reflect shortcut reliance, an incomplete expected feature set, or a downstream effect rather than an incorrect explanation.

\subsection{Statistical Inference}
\label{sec:nulls}

\paragraph{Shuffled-label reference}
A large attribution share does not necessarily indicate mechanism alignment, as unusual sensor values may receive substantial attribution even when a model has learned no meaningful relationship with failure labels. To account for this, we randomly shuffle the overall failure labels $y$ while keeping the sensor measurements $x$, mechanism indicators $z$, and expected feature sets $E_m$ unchanged. For each permutation, we repeat the training and explanation pipeline using the selected model settings and calculate the three audit statistics. The resulting distributions provide a reference for testing whether the observed attribution patterns exceed those obtained after disrupting the input--label relationship.

\paragraph{Significance testing}
For each statistic $T\in\{R_m,D_m,C_m\}$, we compare its observed value with the corresponding values from $K$ shuffled-label pipelines. The one-sided Monte Carlo $p$-value is calculated as

\begin{equation}
p=\frac{1+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{I}(T^{(k)}\geq T)}{K+1}.
\label{eq:pvalue}
\end{equation}

The primary audit uses $K=1{,}000$ permutations. To account for testing multiple mechanisms, we apply Holm's correction~\cite{holm1979simple} across the four physical mechanisms separately for each statistic, using a significance level of $\alpha=0.05$.

\paragraph{Audit Verdicts}
Following the decision process in \cref{fig:pipeline}, we first examine the outside-set statistic $C_m$. A significant result produces a \emph{flagged} verdict, indicating unexpected attribution that requires further investigation. Otherwise, a mechanism is considered \emph{aligned} only when both $R_m$ and $D_m$ are significant. If these conditions are not satisfied, the mechanism is classified as \emph{not aligned}, indicating insufficient statistical evidence of agreement under the tested configuration. Importantly, alignment does not establish physical causality, and the absence of an outside-set flag does not guarantee that the model is free of shortcuts.

To supplement the significance tests, we report standardized differences from the null distribution, $z=(T-\bar{T}_0)/s_0$, where $\bar{T}_0$ and $s_0$ are the null mean and standard deviation. For the primary AI4I audit, we also report 95\% bootstrap intervals for $R_m$ and $D_m$ using 5{,}000 resamples. These intervals describe uncertainty conditional on the fitted models, while the shuffled-label distributions provide the reference for statistical testing. Implementation details and pseudocode are available in the reproduction artifacts~\cite{artifact}.

\section{Experimental Design}

The released labels contain inconsistencies: 24 observations have multiple mechanism flags, nine labeled failures have none, and 18 of the 19 RNF observations are labeled as non-failures. \subsection{Feature Construction}
\label{sec:features}

Several failure rules combine sensor measurements. We therefore construct mechanical power $P$, temperature difference $\Delta T$, overstrain $O$, and overstrain ratio $Q$:

\begin{align}
P &= \tau\left(\frac{2\pi n}{60}\right),\\
\Delta T &= T_{\mathrm{process}}-T_{\mathrm{air}},\\
O &= w\tau,\\
Q &= \frac{O}{L(t)}.
\end{align}

Here, $\tau$, $n$, and $w$ denote torque, rotational speed, and tool wear, while $L(t)$ is the type-specific overstrain limit of 11{,}000, 12{,}000, or 13{,}000 for L, M, and H. Each expected set $E_m$ contains the measurements and derived quantities used to express its failure rule. These features use no failure labels but encode known benchmark rules; we therefore also audit the original features alone to assess their influence on the results.


\subsection{Model Selection and Held-Out Explanations}
\label{sec:training}

For AI4I, we use stratified training, validation, and test splits of 70\%, 20\%, and 10\%. Preprocessing includes scaling and one-hot encoding fitted on the training data. Borderline-SMOTE~\cite{han2005borderline} is selected among imbalance treatments using validation F1. XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} hyperparameters are selected through 40-iteration randomized search with five-fold cross-validation, with oversampling performed inside each training fold. The decision threshold is chosen on the validation set, and the reserved test set measures predictive performance.

For the audit, these choices are fixed and the complete pipeline is refitted using five-fold stratified cross-fitting over all observations. Each observation is explained by a model that did not train on it. The primary audit uses path-dependent TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2020treeshap}; shuffled-label pipelines repeat preprocessing, oversampling, fitting, cross-fitting, and explanation generation. Model selection is not nested within the audit folds, so we separately examine the effect of re-tuning on each permutation. Other classifiers and explanation methods are included in the sensitivity experiments, with full settings reported in the reproduction artifacts~\cite{artifact}.

\subsection{Evaluation Settings Across Datasets}
\label{sec:otherdata}

\paragraph{AI4I variants and shortcuts}
We vary feature construction, imbalance treatment, model family, explanation method, attribution reference, and null-model training, comparing each configuration with its own shuffled-label reference. To introduce a shortcut, we add $s_i=z_{i,\mathrm{HDF}}+\epsilon_i$, where $\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. This noisy copy of the HDF indicator has no physical role and is excluded from $E_{\mathrm{HDF}}$. We vary $\sigma$ from 2 to 0.1 and examine shortcuts distributed over several sensors. Scaling experiments generate up to $3\times10^5$ observations from the documented AI4I process. Variants use $K=200$ permutations unless stated otherwise; the shortcut sweep uses $K=500$.

\paragraph{CWRU bearing recordings}
We use 36 laboratory vibration recordings~\cite{cwru_data} covering inner-race, ball, and outer-race faults. A random forest classifies fault location from 19 features extracted from 4{,}096-sample windows. Expected features are spectral bands around each fault's characteristic defect frequency and second harmonic, with a primary bandwidth of $\pm0.15$ shaft orders. Four-fold group cross-fitting holds out complete recordings, and the null shuffles labels across recordings ($K=1{,}000$). SHAP values explain each observation's recorded fault class, with the other fault classes forming its comparison group. We also introduce a fault-dependent acquisition-gain shortcut that affects vibration amplitude.

\paragraph{Tennessee Eastman process}
We use 400 simulated runs~\cite{reinartz2021tep} containing reactor-cooling (IDV4) and condenser-cooling (IDV5) disturbances. Each run contributes 16 observations before and 16 after fault activation, using 53 process variables; the expected sets contain three variables associated with each disturbed cooling loop. XGBoost uses five-fold group cross-fitting over complete runs. The primary null shuffles fault-activity labels within runs ($K=200$), while a second null rotates the ordered labels to preserve a contiguous fault-active block. We also examine 30-minute temporal features and an LSTM explained with Integrated Gradients. Neither temporal permutation scheme alone establishes exchangeability of the labels.

\paragraph{Audit Component Ablations}
\label{sec:naive}
\label{sec:compare}

We examine the importance of three audit components: held-out explanations, a retrained shuffled-label reference, and the outside-set statistic. A model trained on shuffled labels fails the proposed alignment tests, but a naive comparison with random failure observations makes all four mechanisms appear significant ($p=0.002$). Similarly, using in-sample explanations produces a TWF alignment verdict ($p=0.004$) that does not hold under held-out evaluation, with its relevant share increasing from 0.19 to 0.35. Finally, the relevant-share test alone misses a strong planted shortcut in the large-sample experiment, whereas the outside-set statistic detects it. These results demonstrate how the three components help prevent misleading conclusions about mechanism alignment.

\paragraph{The TWF attribution reference}

\begin{figure}[t]

TWF provides the clearest example of attribution-reference dependence (\cref{fig:twfref}). It is not aligned under path-dependent TreeSHAP, which measures attributions against the oversampled training data. Against that reference, ordinary sensor values count as evidence against failure and shrink tool wear's share of absolute attribution. Measured against normal operation, the same model passes and tool wear's share more than doubles. Oversampling alone is not the full story: an oversampled interventional background also passes, although its share of 0.23 stays close to the path-dependent 0.19. A failure-only reference removes the alignment. Additional checks suggest that feature correlation alone does not explain the difference. The evidence therefore points to the attribution reference as an important factor, without establishing it as the sole cause.

These findings show that an audit verdict should be interpreted together with its model, explanation method, and attribution reference. A change in verdict does not necessarily mean the underlying model has changed how it uses a feature.

The planted-shortcut experiments test whether the audit can reveal misleading reliance without a loss in predictive performance. As the shortcut becomes stronger, its attribution increases while HDF's relevant share decreases (\cref{fig:sweep}). Weak shortcuts ($\sigma\geq0.35$) leave HDF aligned in this sweep, although $\sigma=0.35$ cost HDF its alignment in two of three generated datasets of the same size (\cref{tab:scale}). At $\sigma=0.30$, the mechanism loses alignment but no outside feature is flagged. Stronger shortcuts ($\sigma\leq0.25$) are flagged by the outside-set test. Test F1 remains between 0.84 and 0.92, showing that predictive performance alone does not reveal this reliance.

Detection becomes harder when the shortcut is distributed across several features. At $\sigma=0.35$, three planted sensors collectively receive 38\% of HDF attribution, but none exceeds 14\% individually. HDF loses alignment without a specific outside-set flag. Because $C_m$ examines the largest individual feature excess, distributed shortcuts can escape detection even when their combined influence is substantial.

The experiments therefore establish both the value and the detection limits of the audit: strong shortcuts can be identified despite high predictive accuracy, while weaker or distributed shortcuts may only reduce alignment or remain undetected.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presented a statistical audit to check whether predictive maintenance explanations agree with known failure mechanisms. The results underline why prediction accuracy alone is not enough to judge this agreement. On AI4I, explanations aligned with heat dissipation, power, and overstrain failure rules under the main settings, while the tool-wear result depended on how explanations were computed and which reference data were used. The choice of statistical comparison also mattered: a random-subset baseline suggested alignment even for a model trained on shuffled labels. In controlled experiments, accurate models could rely on fabricated sensors. The audit detected stronger shortcuts but sometimes missed weaker ones or reliance spread across several features. Tests on CWRU bearing recordings and Tennessee Eastman simulations supported alignment for inner- and outer-race bearing faults and the reactor-cooling fault, respectively, under the main settings. Larger-data experiments also found that expected features could receive substantial attribution while another feature still triggered a warning. These results support checking both agreement with failure mechanisms and reliance on other features, while interpreting the findings in light of the model, explanation method, attribution reference, and features considered relevant. Agreement does not establish physical causality, and a model can still use shortcuts that the audit does not detect.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits_future}

% \section{Limitations and Future Work}
% \label{sec:limits_future}

The proposed audit evaluates whether explanations agree with a predefined set of mechanism features under a specified attribution method and statistical reference. Its conclusions therefore depend on what is known about each mechanism and on how the explanations are computed. Significant outside-set attribution identifies features that require further investigation, but does not establish shortcut reliance or physical causality. Our evaluation uses simulated industrial processes and controlled laboratory recordings, where documented failure mechanisms provide a reference for systematic testing. Although these settings demonstrate the audit's applicability, they do not establish its performance on operating industrial equipment. Future work will focus on validating the framework using real-world maintenance data with independently verified failure causes and developing practical methods for defining expected feature sets in more complex industrial systems.

% The audit requires predefined expected feature sets, which may be incomplete or difficult to establish for complex industrial systems. Its conclusions also depend on the attribution reference, training configuration, and assumptions of the shuffled-label procedure. An outside-set flag may reflect a shortcut, an omitted mechanism feature, or a downstream process response, while weak or distributed shortcuts may remain undetected. These limitations are particularly relevant when failures affect several interacting variables or when temporal dependencies complicate the statistical reference.

% Our evaluation is limited to simulated failure processes and controlled laboratory recordings. Agreement with their documented mechanisms does not establish agreement with verified causes in operating machinery. Future work should therefore examine industrial data with independently established failure causes, develop more complete mechanism-feature maps, and improve detection of distributed shortcuts. Better treatment of temporal dependencies, principled effect-size thresholds, and more efficient null-model evaluation would also strengthen the audit's reliability and practical use.
